## What could connect hotel with lodging?

In [Chapter 9](../ch-09/), `hotel allowance` failed even though the collection contained relevant lodging policies. Keyword, Count and TF-IDF all needed overlap in the document vocabulary. Increasing an IDF cannot create a shared coordinate between unrelated spellings. The failure suggests a different question: can occurrences in similar surroundings teach different words to share useful numerical features?

We should specify what would count as improvement. For **retrieval**, use the same five KA-0 documents, nine queries, relevance labels and abstention rule. For **language prediction**, score the same next-token events under each compared language model. A retrieval rank cannot be substituted for a language-model probability, and a lower perplexity cannot by itself establish better retrieval.

This chapter first inspects a deliberately supplied static representation, then explains training signals that can learn representations, and finally follows a small neural language model from IDs to loss. The supplied vectors, actual training run and cited research objectives are separate kinds of evidence.

## Replace vocabulary coordinates with shared features

A BoW vector has one coordinate for each vocabulary term. Another arrangement assigns each word a shorter numerical row, then combines the rows of a document. The same coordinate can contribute to many words; it no longer names one spelling. This is a **distributed representation**. When most entries are stored as ordinary numerical coordinates rather than a huge set of mostly absent vocabulary terms, it is commonly called a **dense embedding**. A dense representation can contain zeros; “dense” does not mean every coordinate is nonzero or that the representation understands meaning.

**Example 10.1 — A complete hand-assigned diagnostic.** To isolate the effect of sharing, give words the following three-coordinate rows. These are authored assumptions, **not trained Word2Vec or GloVe output**. We deliberately call the coordinates lodging/location, order, and rail for this example only; learned embedding axes do not automatically have those names.

**Table 10.1 — All non-default rows in the supplied static map.** Unassigned terms, including the two amounts, contribute `[0,0,0]`.

| Words | Supplied row |
| --- | --- |
| Beijing, lodging, hotel, allowance | `[1,0,0]` |
| order, processing, approval | `[0,1,0]` |
| train | `[0,0,1]` |
| 600, 750 | `[0,0,0]` |

Represent a card by the arithmetic mean of its token rows, counting zero rows in the denominator. A therefore becomes `[2/3,0,0]`, B becomes `[3/4,0,0]`, C becomes `[3/4,0,1/4]`, and D/E both become `[0,1,0]`. For `hotel allowance`, the query mean is `[1,0,0]`. Cosine against A and B is 1, against C is $3/\sqrt{10}\approx0.948683$, and against D/E is zero. The tie rule chooses A.

That is a successful answer to query 4's **any-version source-finding** contract: A or B is relevant. It is not a successful certification of the current 750-yuan amount. Indeed, the same map collapses A and B to the same direction and maps processing and approval to the same row. It fixes one lexical miss while destroying distinctions needed elsewhere. Reducing eight coordinates to three is not evidence of better semantics.

The representation is **static** because a word's row stays the same across its occurrences once the map is fixed. A document mean can still change when other words change. What it cannot do is give the individual word a different row according to its current sense. We need training evidence to choose useful rows, and later we will need a way to use context more directly.

## Use neighboring words as a learning signal

The **distributional hypothesis** motivates a useful kind of evidence: words used in similar linguistic environments often share aspects of meaning or function. Suppose `hotel` and `lodging` both occur near `room` and `night`. Training that asks their representations to help predict those neighbors may encourage shared features. This is a tendency to exploit, not a theorem of synonymy. Opposites can appear in similar contexts, and a topic association is not equivalence.

**Word2Vec** names a family of word-representation training architectures. In **continuous bag of words (CBOW)**, surrounding word representations are combined to predict a center word; in **skip-gram**, a center word helps predict surrounding words. These contexts may include both left and right neighbors. That differs from a left-to-right language model predicting an unseen next token from its available past. [Mikolov et al., 2013, §§3.1–3.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1301.3781)

**Example 10.2 — See what a skip-gram prediction rewards.** Consider a reduced, supplied training situation: the center is `hotel`, with input row $v=[1,0]$; the only possible context targets are `room` and `train`, whose output rows are $u_{\rm room}=[\ln3,0]$ and $u_{\rm train}=[0,0]$. Dot products give scores `[ln 3,0]`. Softmax gives `[3/4,1/4]`. If the observed neighbor is room, the loss is $-\ln(3/4)=\ln(4/3)\approx0.287682$.

This training signal favors raising the observed neighbor's score relative to the alternative. Through Chapter 7's chain rule, the loss can change the center input row and the context output rows. If another center word repeatedly predicts similar neighbors, the shared prediction task provides a route for statistical sharing. The numbers above demonstrate an objective under supplied values; no actual Word2Vec training is claimed for them.

In full-softmax skip-gram, the general target probability has the form

$$
P(o\mid i)=\frac{\exp(u_o\cdot v_i)}{\sum_{j\in\mathcal V}\exp(u_j\cdot v_i)}.
$$

$i$ names the center word, $o$ the observed neighbor, and $\mathcal V$ the context-target vocabulary. Input rows $v$ and output rows $u$ play different roles even when the vocabulary spellings are the same. Substituting the two rows above reproduces $3/(3+1)=3/4$. Summing over a large vocabulary is costly. **Negative sampling** uses a different contrastive objective, distinguishing observed pairs from selected noise pairs; it should not be read as a normalized next-token distribution or used directly to report language-model perplexity. [Mikolov et al., 2013, §2](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2013/file/9aa42b31882ec039965f3c4923ce901b-Paper.pdf)

**GloVe** supplies another learning signal. It first collects word–context co-occurrence counts, then fits dot products and biases to logarithms of those counts. With an authored count of 4 for hotel–room and 1 for hotel–train, the corresponding fitting targets are $\ln4$ and $\ln1=0$, not probabilities 4 and 1. Its objective has the form

$$
J=\sum_{i,j:X_{ij}>0}f(X_{ij})\big(v_i\cdot u_j+b_i+\tilde b_j-\ln X_{ij}\big)^2.
$$

$X_{ij}$ is a co-occurrence count under a declared context rule; the vectors and biases are fitted parameters. The expression in parentheses is a prediction error in log-count units, squared to penalize either sign. $f$ weights count ranges rather than allowing every count to dominate equally. The zero-count terms are omitted before taking logarithms, consistent with zero weight at zero; we do not calculate `log(0)` and then multiply infinity by zero. This explains how global co-occurrence evidence trains static rows, without reproducing the paper's large-corpus experiment. [Pennington et al., 2014, §3](https://aclanthology.org/D14-1162.pdf)

## Track a neural language model through one whole prediction

A **neural language model (NNLM)** can instead learn embeddings jointly with next-token prediction. Replace Chapter 4's separate context-count rows with shared numerical operations: look up the last few tokens, combine them, compute hidden values, produce logits and score the known next token. Bengio and colleagues' model establishes this fixed-window, jointly learned representation approach. Our following network is an original small simplification, not a reproduction of their empirical settings. [Bengio et al., 2003, §2](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/volume3/bengio03a/bengio03a.pdf)

**Example 10.3 — A fully supplied two-token-window prediction.** Use vocabulary order `beijing, lodging, 750, EOS`, IDs 0–3. The context is `beijing lodging`, with IDs `[0,1]`; the supplied next-token target is ID 2, `750`. This four-entry paper vocabulary is separate from Chapter 3's tokenizer and from the measured run below. All values are hand assigned, dimensionless parameters.

The lookup table is

$$
E=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\\1&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Each of the four token types has two coordinates. For one example, IDs have shape `[B,W]=[1,2]`, where $W=2$ is the window length. Lookup produces rows `[[1,0],[0,1]]` with shape `[B,W,C]=[1,2,2]`. Concatenate them **in position order** to get $x=[1,0,0,1]$, shape `[1,4]`. Concatenation appends coordinates; it neither adds the vectors nor adds the IDs.

Supply a hidden-layer matrix and bias:

$$
H=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\\0&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix},\qquad b=[0,0].
$$

$H$ has shape `[W×C,J]=[4,2]`, with $J=2$ hidden coordinates. The first output is $1(1)+0(0)+0(0)+1(0)=1$; the second is $1(0)+0(0)+0(0)+1(1)=1$. Adding the zero bias and applying ReLU leaves $h=[1,1]$, shape `[1,2]`.

Finally supply

$$
U=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\ln3&0\\0&0&0&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad d=[0,0,0,0].
$$

$U$ has shape `[J,V]=[2,4]` and $d$ has one entry per output candidate. The logits are $z=hU+d=[0,0,\ln3,0]$, shape `[1,4]`. Their exponentials are `[1,1,3,1]`, total 6. The target probability is $3/6=1/2$, and target loss is $\ln2\approx0.693147$. Every matrix, bias, input ID and target used in that computation has now been specified.

```book-figure

```

Figure 10.1 preserves the distinction between a position axis and a coordinate axis before flattening. The general path is $h=\operatorname{ReLU}(xH+b)$ followed by $z=hU+d$. Backpropagation can update $E,H,U,b,d$ together. The lookup selects parameter rows; it does not differentiate a token's integer ID. A shared row receives contributions from every occurrence that uses it.

Reverse the context to `lodging beijing`. Lookup now concatenates `[0,1,1,0]`; multiplying by this supplied $H$ gives `[0,0]`, all logits become zero and the target probability becomes $1/4$. The average of the two input rows would have remained `[1/2,1/2]`. Concatenation can preserve order inside the window, while mean pooling cannot.

## Run the small model, keeping its task separate from retrieval

The actual CPU experiment uses the five KA-0 index cards as five training sequences of canonical word symbols. The locale tokenizers map their authored translations to these symbols; this is not evidence of learning English–Chinese translation. Each sequence begins with two input-only BOS markers and ends with one scored EOS. There are 20 training events. No context crosses a document boundary.

The output vocabulary is `EOS, beijing, lodging, 600, 750, train, order, processing, approval`, IDs 0–8. The input table adds `BOS` as ID 9. Thus the trained table has shape `[10,4]`, distinct from Example 10.3's `[4,2]` table. With a batch of 20 events, the measured network follows `[20,2] → [20,2,4] → [20,8] → [20,8] → [20,9]`: IDs, embeddings, concatenation, hidden values, logits. Targets have shape `[20]`. BOS is never an output candidate; EOS is scored. The table contributes $10\times4=40$ parameters, the hidden layer $8\times8+8=72$, and the output layer $8\times9+9=81$, for $40+72+81=193$ trainable numbers.

**Table 10.2 — Frozen language-prediction splits.** Each displayed sequence also scores its final EOS. These short variants are authored diagnostics, not a representative language corpus.

| Split | Sequences | Scored events |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Train | The five complete index cards in Table 9.1 | 20 |
| Validation | `beijing lodging 750`; `order order processing` | 8 |
| Test | `beijing lodging lodging 600`; `beijing train` | 8 |

We use seed 23, CPU float64, one thread, embedding dimension 4, hidden width 8, ReLU, and Adam at learning rate 0.03 for 200 full-batch updates. The first minimum of validation mean loss selects step 10: train loss 1.258041, validation loss 1.454214. The two test sequences are scored only after that selection. This is a completed local run; the record includes all intermediate losses, model values, hashes and elapsed time. No pretrained weights or model account is used.

Check one count fraction independently. In training, context `beijing lodging` is followed once by 600 and once by lodging. Add-one smoothing gives the target lodging numerator 2 and denominator $2+9=11$. For `lodging lodging`, only 750 was observed, so an unseen 600 receives $1/(1+9)=1/10$. The shared fixture preserves every count fraction before the neural code runs.

The NNLM gives **1.424429 nats**, perplexity **4.155483**, and **5/8** correct top choices. Its better average likelihood coexists with fewer correct top choices: it puts much more probability on some observed targets, including several EOS events, while missing others. Neither result establishes better current-policy answering. These are language-prediction metrics over eight particular events.

## A static row merges senses; a window can lose the clue entirely

The English word `train` provides a simple ambiguity: it can refer to a rail vehicle or to fitting a model. Our actual corpus uses only its rail sense. A single frozen row cannot switch senses when the same spelling appears in `train a model`. Other words may change a sentence's mean, but the train row itself is unchanged. Word2Vec and GloVe also assign static rows in their basic forms; training on a larger corpus can mix senses in that row rather than selecting one per occurrence.

A fixed-window NNLM has a different limitation. It can use different combinations of words inside its window, but a clue outside that window is absent from its input. Increasing hidden width or training longer cannot recover information that the input mapping has made identical.

**Example 10.4 — Prove a window collision without blaming unknown words.** Use a separate declared vocabulary `red, blue, filler, key, is`, IDs 0–4. A diagnostic prompt states the key's color at the start, repeats six identical filler tokens, then asks with the final words `key is`. The required output color is the one stated at the start. The two ID sequences are `[0,2,2,2,2,2,2,3,4]` and `[1,2,2,2,2,2,2,3,4]`; targets are red and blue respectively.

With window length 2, both inputs become `[3,4]`. Fixed parameters therefore produce the same output distribution for both, whatever the hidden width. This is a representational impossibility under the stated interface, not a measured failure of our KA vocabulary on unfamiliar words. For a deterministic top-color rule, the model cannot answer both correctly. Randomly choosing different outputs does not make the choices depend on the discarded clue.

Figure 10.2 places the two different clues outside the identical final windows. A larger window postpones the boundary and enlarges the concatenated input and its following matrix. It still has a finite boundary. The motivating question for Chapter 11 is now specific: can we carry a state forward as tokens arrive, so earlier information need not be supplied as a fixed-length concatenation? That is where recurrence belongs; this chapter does not assume it has already been built.

## Practice record and chapter summary

`05-representations-and-nnlm.ipynb` reproduces the supplied paper calculation, trains the fixed CPU model, verifies its held-out count fractions, and compares both static methods on unchanged KA-0 queries. Shared inputs are `nnlm-paper.json`, `nnlm-data.json` and `ka-static-v1.json`; actual outputs and selected parameters are in `nnlm-run.json` and `nnlm-model.json`. The companion README records versions, seeds, candidate orders, precision and execution commands. The original static map is clearly separated from the trained snapshot.

Sparse lexical methods assign coordinates to spellings. Learned embeddings can share features across words because a training objective connects their occurrences, but usefulness depends on data and task. Word2Vec neighbor prediction and GloVe co-occurrence fitting are representation objectives; a fixed-window NNLM predicts a next token and learns its input rows jointly. Mean-pooled retrieval and sequential prediction remain different computations with different metrics. Static senses and missing earlier context are concrete limits, not reasons to declare every dense representation better.

## Reference answers

1. Both means are `[0,1,0]`, so their cosine is 1. The map assigned processing and approval the same row and the averaging cannot distinguish them. Renaming axes does not change any value or restore lost information; a different representation or task rule needs to retain the distinction.
2. Train has probability $1/4$, so its loss is $\ln4\approx1.386294$, compared with $\ln(4/3)$ for room. The observed target is now the low-scoring candidate. Its score should rise relative to room's; the same input vector can receive conflicting contributions from different observed neighbors, which training must combine.
3. Concatenation is `[0,1,1,0]`; the supplied hidden matrix gives `[0,0]`, all four logits are zero, target probability is $1/4$ and loss is $\ln4$. For batch size 3, the shapes are `[3,2]`, `[3,2,2]`, `[3,4]`, `[3,4]`. The two final shapes happen to match, but one axis names concatenated input coordinates and the other output candidates.
4. The likelihood comparison is against a count language model on eight held-out target events: NNLM perplexity is 4.155483 versus 5.475957, while top-choice counts are 5/8 versus 6/8. Retrieval is a separate nine-query comparison: both NNLM-row means and TF-IDF succeed on 7/9. Neither number supports the claimed retrieval improvement, and neither tiny diagnostic establishes a population-wide result.
5. No. Both visible contexts remain `[3,4]`, so any deterministic network with fixed parameters receives identical input. It must receive a larger context containing the clue or a carried representation that preserves relevant earlier information. More hidden coordinates alone cannot reconstruct an input difference that was removed.
6. A supplied plot demonstrates only relationships under authored values; trained claims require an actual objective, data, configuration and run evidence. Negative sampling scores distinguish observed/noise pairs and are not automatically normalized next-token probabilities. A perplexity comparison requires a proper declared output distribution and exactly matched token events, vocabulary and boundary rules.
7. Cosine 1 establishes matching direction under the chosen representation. Check whether the question asks for current or historical policy, whether the source version is superseded, and which effective-date rule applies. B supports 750 for the declared current date. An amount answer requires that source evidence; an order action separately requires its authorized operation and approval path. A retrieval score supplies neither applicability nor permission.
